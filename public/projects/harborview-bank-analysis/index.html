Harborview Bank Power BI Analysis

A Power BI analysis that settles a CFO-vs-Retail argument: fee income hit a record, but East-region credit losses turned the bank's 2025 result negative.

Output

The business question

Leadership disagreed about 2025. The Head of Retail called it the best year for fee income, thanks to the July 2024 rise in the monthly checking fee from $5 to $12. The CFO said overall results got worse, with customers leaving and credit losses rising.

The analysis tests both claims and recommends whether to keep the fee, and what to do about customers and credit risk in 2026. Before looking at any values, each claim was turned into neutral questions (an issue tree), with 14 KPIs and written hypotheses.

What the data showed

  1. Fee income rose 2.6×, from $0.45M (2023) to $1.16M (2025), because of the price, not more customers: accounts paying the fee fell from 8,803 to 7,994.
  2. The price rise very likely cost customers. Mass checking closures went from 0.39% to 3.05% a month in the first nine months at $12, while fee-free customers stayed flat. The jump started the week of the change, hit only checking, and appeared in every region: about 2,640 extra closures by December 2025.
  3. The fee is still a net gain, but a shrinking one: about +$743K after an estimated $0.29M of lost revenue, with the monthly gain falling from $59K to $32K.
  4. The measurable result fell from +$2.56M to −$0.41M because gross credit losses rose ninefold, from $0.52M to $4.65M, not because of the fee.
  5. Three quarters of 2025 credit losses came from the East region. East branches roughly tripled lending from early 2024, about half of it to borrowers scoring under 650; their 2024 loans defaulted 10.5% of the time within a year, against 2.1% elsewhere.

Verdict: both leaders were partly right. Fee income did reach a record, but credit losses in East turned the result negative.

Data cleaning in Power Query

Every column was profiled on the full data set, and 19 problems across four tables were fixed and logged, each with how it was found and how many rows it affected. For example:

  • 120 duplicate customers, 20 duplicate loans and 9,279 duplicate transactions.
  • Two date formats in one column: a plain type change would have silently swapped day and month on 549 rows.
  • Interest rates stored as percentages instead of decimals, and placeholder credit scores of 0 and 999.
  • Transactions dated after the extract date, and 150 transactions on accounts that don't exist.

Data model

A star schema in Import mode, with auto date/time and automatic relationship detection turned off. Every relationship is defined by hand, one-to-many and single-direction.

TableRowsRole
Transactions2,319,645Fact: payments, fees and transfers
Loans5,300Fact: amount, rate, balance, status
Accounts36,605Dimension between Customers and Transactions
Customers20,000Dimension: segment, region, income band
Branches25Dimension: branch, city, region
Products6Dimension: checking, savings, card, three loan types
Date2,557Marked date table, 2019–2025

Two design decisions worth calling out:

  • Two dates on Loans, one active. The issue date drives volume and default rate by vintage; the default date is an inactive relationship switched on with USERELATIONSHIP inside the loss measures.
  • No Branches → Customers relationship. It would create two filter paths to Loans (issuing branch vs. home branch), so customer geography uses Customers[Region] instead.

DAX measures & validation

Measures are grouped into folders with descriptions, and include month-end customer snapshots, churn and reactivation flows, a 12-month default rate by loan vintage, and a control-group estimate of the closures caused by the price change.

Every KPI was reconciled against totals calculated outside Power BI, and against identities that must hold every month: fee income = paying accounts × average fee, and customers at end = start + new + returning − leavers. All checks return zero and are shown on a dedicated Data Checks page in the report.

Recommendations & limits

  1. Tighten credit approval in East now: bring the share of sub-650 loans back to the 12–16% seen elsewhere and review late East loans one by one.
  2. Keep the $12 fee, but protect the Mass base: record why customers leave and test a fee waiver for active customers on a random group first.
  3. Investigate the July 2025 chargebacks ($373K, not confirmed as fraud) and fix the data gaps.

The report never calls the result “profit”: the data has no operating costs, deposit interest or loan-loss provisions, and losses are gross because there is no recovery data. Harborview is a fictional bank on a simulated dataset; no real customer information is used.
